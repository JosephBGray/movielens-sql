# MovieLens SQL Analysis

A SQL portfolio project analyzing the MovieLens 32M dataset: 32 million movie ratings and 2 million tag applications across ~87,500 movies, generated by ~201,000 users between 1995 and 2023.

**Tools:** MySQL &nbsp;|&nbsp; **Dataset:** [MovieLens 32M (GroupLens Research, University of Minnesota)](https://grouplens.org/datasets/movielens/32m/)

---

## Dataset

The dataset is split across 4 files (`movies.csv`, `ratings.csv`, `tags.csv`, and `links.csv`), joined primarily through `movieId`, with `userId` linking ratings and tags.

Data provided by GroupLens Research ([Harper & Konstan, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2827872)). The raw CSVs are not included in this repo. Download them from the GroupLens link above.

## Data Cleaning Summary

- **Duplicates:** Checked all 4 tables for duplicate primary/composite keys. `movies`, `links`, and `ratings` were clean. `tags` flagged 4 apparent duplicates on `userId` + `movieId` + `tag`, but manual inspection showed these were false positives: MySQL's default collation treats visually similar strings (accented characters, full-width vs. half-width punctuation) as equal. All 4 rows were genuinely distinct and were kept.
- **NULLs and empty strings:** Audited every table's key join and filter columns. All came back clean.
- **Release year and title:** Extracted `release_year` and `title_clean` from the title field (formatted like `"Toy Story (1995)"`). Debugging the extraction surfaced titles with trailing whitespace after the year, which broke an end-anchored regex. Traced this to the byte level with `HEX()`, fixed it with `TRIM()`, and resolved the final two edge cases manually. 615 movies (~0.7%) had no year in the title at all and were left as NULL rather than guessed.
- **Genre normalization:** Split the pipe-separated `genres` column into a `movie_genres` junction table (one row per movie-genre pair) using a recursive CTE, so genres can be properly filtered, joined, and aggregated.
- **Hidden-character bug:** Investigating why the `(no genres listed)` placeholder wasn't matching revealed that every row in `movies.genres` ended in a hidden carriage return (`\r`), left over from the CSV's Windows line endings. Stripped it from the column and rebuilt `movie_genres` from the corrected data.
- **Ratings validity:** Confirmed all 32 million ratings fall within the 0.5–5.0 range on half-star increments.

## Business Questions & Key Findings

### 1. Which genres have the highest average rating vs. the highest volume of ratings?

- Popularity and rating tend to move in opposite directions. Mainstream, high-volume genres (Comedy, Action, Sci-Fi) rank much lower on average rating, while niche genres (Film-Noir, Documentary, War) rate notably higher despite far fewer ratings.
- **Film-Noir** is the most extreme case: #1 in average rating but last (#19) in volume.
- **Drama** is the standout exception: the single most-rated genre, yet still #5 in average rating.
- A likely explanation is self-selection. Niche-genre viewers seek out movies they already expect to like, while mainstream genres draw a broader, less selective audience.

### 2. Has average rating / rating volume changed over the decades?

- Ratings volume peaks sharply for 1990s movies and tapers off in later decades, even though the catalog contains *more* titles from the 2000s and 2010s.
- Normalizing to ratings per movie confirms the gap is real, not a byproduct of catalog size: **~1,357 ratings/movie for the 1990s vs. ~165 for the 2010s**.
- The most likely explanation is that MovieLens user activity was concentrated in that earlier period, though the dataset can't directly confirm the platform's usage history.

### 3. Do a small group of "power users" drive a disproportionate share of ratings?

- Yes, substantially. The top 10% most active users account for **47%** of all ratings, and the top 1% alone account for **26.6%**.
- The mean of ~160 ratings per user is inflated by this concentration, so the median gives a more honest picture of a typical user.
- The dataset reflects a smaller, highly engaged population rather than general audience opinion, which is worth keeping in mind when interpreting any aggregate in this project.
- **Sensitivity check:** Recalculating the Question 1 genre ratings without the top 1% of raters produced the same pattern (Film-Noir highest-rated and lowest-volume, Drama the mainstream exception). The genre trend reflects broad user behavior rather than a small group of enthusiasts.

### 4. What are the most commonly applied tags, and do genres attract different tag vocabularies?

- The most common tags skew toward mood and craft descriptors (*atmospheric*, *thought-provoking*, *visually appealing*, *surreal*) rather than plot content, consistent with an engaged, film-literate user base.
- *Atmospheric* (#2 overall) was applied by 2,205 distinct users, confirming broad usage rather than a few prolific taggers.
- These broadly applicable tags show up among the top tags across many genres, which explains their high overall totals.
- Niche genres still carry distinct vocabularies. For example, *Quentin Tarantino* is a top-5 tag for Western, and *neo-noir* is a top-5 tag for Film-Noir.

## Skills Demonstrated

Multi-table joins · window functions (`RANK`, `NTILE`, partitioned ranking) · recursive CTEs · aggregation · regex text parsing (`REGEXP_SUBSTR`, `REGEXP_REPLACE`) · byte-level debugging (`HEX`) · data cleaning (duplicate/NULL audits, collation troubleshooting, hidden-character bugs) · statistical reasoning (mean vs. median, sensitivity testing) · bias-aware interpretation of findings

## How to Run

1. Download MovieLens 32M from GroupLens and load the 4 CSVs into a MySQL schema named `movielens`.
2. Run `MovieLens_Data_Cleaning.sql` top to bottom.
3. Run the queries in `MovieLens_Business_Questions.sql`.
